Section 1

System components and how they connect

The core is simple: the client talks to the server over REST and listens to game events, the server keeps games in PostgreSQL and reads the rules of the game from JSON reference files on disk. Everything else in the diagram is the workshop: tools that measure, shake and teach the very same game.

Rule
Data, not code

A new building, technology, gun, hull or leader is a JSON entry, not a branch in a service. The database keeps only their codes.

Rule
One calculation, one place

All of MoO2's numeric rules live in PopulationCalculator, combat in BattleRules, flight in FlightRules. A value computed twice will one day disagree with itself.

Rule
A turn is read once

The galaxy is loaded once per turn and handed to the phases as a context. A query "per player" inside a turn hits the whole turn.

Section 2

How the balancing instruments work together

They share a single task: to set a fair price for every race trait. There are about fifty of them, and the player assembles a race from them on a fixed budget. A price is not set by eye: it is measured, in four stages, each of which checks the previous one.

Why the assessor needs a breaker

The console sets prices from random builds, but a live player does not play a random one: they look for the strongest. A combination that beats everything else cannot be caught with random builds — it turns up once in a thousand. The oracle hunts for it on purpose and answers two questions of the plan: is there a dominant build, and which traits never made it to the top.

Seven measures of strength

Production and research with a weight of 1; money, military strength, ground combat, breadth of research and espionage with a weight of ½. One measure is not enough: in MOO II a colonist is put either at the workbench or in the lab, and without a research measure "poor scientists" at −3 points came out as a bargain (+1.19); with it they fell into place (−3.43).

Subtlety
Weight comes from regression

Traits are not bought one at a time, and a strong one would drag along everything that usually sits next to it. The share is centred to zero within a game, and the starting position goes in as columns.

Subtlety
A combo has its own price

Trait prices add up, while the strength of some pairs multiplies. So a pair carries a surcharge — and it is measured too, not assigned.

Subtlety
Empty ≠ zero

A measure that never fired in a game returns empty. Zero for everyone means "nothing to measure with", not "everyone is equally weak".

Section 3

Neural network: structure and training

The network learns to play our game, not the original, and the reason is simple: here a game is computed over REST in seconds, while in DOSBox it is half an hour of a real mouse. Thousands of games there cannot be played in any amount of time.

Each head was added not for completeness but after a measurement showed that without it there was nothing to learn. A network that could only build never once left its home system in sixty turns — one colony, zero fleets; a network that did not touch the colonists played with whatever distribution the colony got at its founding.

Section 4

Development method: capturing scenes from the original

Half of MOO II's windows are not published anywhere, and their layout cannot be restored from memory. So the layout of our screens is checked against screenshots of the real game taken in DOSBox. The tools for this grew into a subsystem of their own.

Why recording a human turned out cheaper than guessing

The tool could not move colonists from one job to another — it never could. This was fixed in four attempts in a row, and each one cost a game: dragging the figure, clicking the figure, clicking an empty slot, trying the whole row. The observations contradicted each other. The project owner stated the rule in one sentence: a click picks up everyone to the right of the point, and the next one drops them where the cursor is. Everything worked on the first try. That is the reckoning the orionometer was born from: a question costs one sentence, while guesswork costs hours and ruined games.

Three pitfalls that broke scene recognition

Signature
Take it from what stays still

The colony signature was taken from the corner with the governor's portrait — and a freshly founded colony was not recognised at all. The buy-out window's was taken from the question line, where the price and amount sit, and those change every time.

Signature
A poor one swallows others

A dark stripe running the full height of the screen matches almost anything: it ended up labelling 50 clicks on 32 different points — in a window with a single button.

Signature
A window over the map is an "overlay"

The map signature was taken from the bottom bar, and that bar shows from under any window. This hid exactly the two things the whole effort was for: sending a fleet and the start of a battle — 37 frames under someone else's name.

The conclusion, written into the project's pitfalls: the absence of something in a log is a statement about the instrument, not about the world.

Section 6

Measuring instruments: the console and the oracle in detail

Both answer one question — what is a race trait worth — and answer it differently. The console measures prices on random builds and passes a verdict on each. The oracle does the opposite: it deliberately hunts for a build that beats everything else. The assessor and the breaker take turns, and whatever the breaker finds goes into the next measurement.

Below is how both work, straight from the code: BalanceRunService, BalanceVerdictRules, RaceBuildGenerator, LeastSquares, BalanceOracleService, BalanceOracleRules. Every number comes from these files, not from general considerations: each one sits there with an explanation of where it came from.

The balancing console — the assessor

The game owner describes a run from the browser: the galaxy, how many empires and what each one plays, how many games and how many turns each, which combo to plant. The server plays them in the background and returns not raw measurements but a verdict on every price.

What the instrument does before it starts counting

  • It snapshots the prices before the first game. A run takes tens of minutes, and prices are edited from the neighbouring screen and directly in the file. The snapshot sits in the run record itself — otherwise a day later nobody could tell which prices it measured.
  • It starts after the transaction commits. The background thread first reads the run record and cannot find it in an uncommitted transaction: the run silently froze with the words "running" and zero games.
  • One at a time — the executor has a single thread. As many games run at once as BalanceLoad allows: full power at night, less in the daytime, and the limit is asked before each game rather than at start — otherwise changing it would mean restarting the server, that is, killing the run in progress.

Where builds come from

RaceBuildGenerator produces a legal build exactly on budget — the same one a player could assemble: no more than one trait from a toggle group, no incompatible pairs, with surcharges for combos. Three decisions inside it matter more than the rest.

Build
It can sell weaknesses

While the generator took only positive traits, the whole negative half of the table was a blind spot: fourteen traits out of fifty-three never made it into a measurement, and their prices had nothing to be checked against.

Build
The refund is chosen in advance

A greedy pick would always take weaknesses — they only free up points — and the refund would almost always hit the ceiling. Then the measurement would compare not "with the weakness and without" but "with this weakness and with that one".

Build
Free picks are filled in at the end

Wherever a group was left empty. This way a build always has a government: every race in the designer has one, dictatorship simply costs nothing. Without it a zero-point build was a race with no government at all — a state a player is never in.

A random build is re-rolled for every game, not once per run: such a sample holds as many observations as there are builds, not empires. And builds are shuffled across seats from game to game — the starting position is the main source of spread, and a build pinned to a seat would take its luck for itself.

Seven measures of strength

MeasureWeightComputed fromWhy that much
PRODUCTION1share of the game's productionthe base: with it the gap between budgets shows confidently, whereas by power it drowned in its own error
RESEARCH1share of the game's researchthe other half of the economy: a colonist goes either to the workbench or to the lab
MONEY0.5income per turna secondary flow: gc turn into production at 2–4 per unit. Not the treasury — that is a reserve
MILITARY0.5strength of the ships builta fleet is not a flow but an investment; whoever built no fleet gets zero — and rightly so
GROUND0.5colonies taken by landing minus colonies lostcaptures alone did not tell fighters apart at all: 0.73 / 0.56 / 1.04 at prices 2 / 1 / −1
TECHNOLOGY0.5number of technologies researchedintroduced for creativity: its benefit is breadth, and the research measure cannot see that difference
ESPIONAGE0.5missions carried out to the endnot a flow but a weapon: a handful of events per game, and the share jumps more than the others

The verdict is passed on the sum (TOTAL), but the console shows the terms as well: "strong" and "strong, but only in research" are different things, and it is the project owner who decides them, not the instrument.

  • The share is centred to zero within a game. The mean is known in advance — one hundred percent divided by the number of empires — and subtracting it removes the galaxy effect entirely, without a separate column per game.
  • Ground combat is handled its own way. It is already the difference between taken and lost, and its mean over a game is identically zero: every capture is someone's loss. Subtracting 100/n from it would punish everyone who did not fight on the ground in that game.
  • Empty is not zero. A measure that never fired in a game gives exactly zero, not minus the mean: otherwise the absence of a measurement would become a measurement.

How a measurement becomes a verdict

Thresholds the verdict is based on

ConstantValueWhat it means
MIN_TAKERS12with fewer takers the trait is not measured at all, and its verdict is "not measured", not "fair"
MAX_SHARE_OF_TAKERS0.5a trait taken by more than half of the builds is not measured: the "non-takers" bought its group neighbour instead, and the comparison is not with absence but with a substitute. Free dictatorship sits in 97 % of builds
ERRORS_TO_JUDGE2.0the usual distinguishability threshold. Under two errors it is the spread of games, not a property of the trait
RIDGE_SHARE0.02the ridge as a share of the number of observations: as the sample grows the shrinkage stays proportionate, and prices do not jump just because a run got longer
MAX_FAIR_PRICE_BUDGETS2.0a fair price beyond two budgets is not shown: that is no longer a careful estimate but noise. The verdict stays — it does not depend on the division
DAMPING0.4a price moves part of the way towards the measured one, not to the point: value depends on price, and a jump would swing prices instead of settling them

The point rate is taken from the measurements themselves — a "price vs. strength" line is fitted over the distinguishable traits, and its slope is the rate. A single rate taken from budgets at stage 1 will not do: it turned out indistinguishable from zero, because strong traits in a random build are cancelled out by weak ones.

The price scale and the value scale diverged, and that is the main finding of stage 2: a point is worth about 0.05 p.p. of production share, so the whole fifteen-point budget is three quarters of a point, while "good industry" alone at four points gives two and a half points — more than three full budgets. That is why the advice is clamped to what the table can express, while the fair price shown is not: let it be visible how far the trait goes beyond the expressible.

Trait combos — the second measurement

For half the table the honest answer is "worth nothing on its own": cyborgs only eat, but together with industry and a hungry world they are the Meklar. Such traits are not bought one at a time, and measuring them one at a time means measuring the wrong thing — not what the player comes to them for. So the measurement gets a column per combo: a one where both traits are taken together, and its weight is exactly what the combo gives on top of its halves.

  • It is the second measurement, not the first. Combo columns take weight away from the traits themselves, while the player pays for a trait whatever build it ends up in. Price verdicts stay with the first measurement.
  • The forty most frequent pairs are taken with a threshold of twelve takers: fifty traits make more than a thousand pairs, and a column for each would make the matrix wider than the sample — the whole measurement would degenerate, not just the combos.
  • The threshold grows with the number of tests. Not one named pair is tested but forty at once, and the most extreme of forty independent measurements drifts from zero by about √(2·ln 40) ≈ 2.7 errors on its own. On the first real run the instrument produced exactly one "find" — a combo that had no reason to exist.
  • The background is subtracted by the median. The ridge shrinks trait weights towards zero, and the combo column picks up the model's residue: on synthetic data with no combo at all the instrument counted ±0.44 against its own error of 0.2. The median bonus across all pairs is the background, and a find is whatever sticks out of it threefold.
  • A triple is not a whim. It has its own column next to the columns of all three pairs inside it, so its weight answers a precise question: does the triple give anything beyond what its pairs and singles already give.
  • Planting is a coin toss per trait — a pair has four quarters, a triple eight eighths. "All or none" would be fatal: the combo column would coincide with the columns of its parts, and none of the weights in such a set would be defined.

The residual — how a round's success is measured

The median gap between a price and what the trait earned, in price points. Not a count of "fair" verdicts: that measures not balance but how blurry a run is. Measured on repeats with the same price table — in one trio of runs the fair verdicts came out at 20, 40 and 27, a spread larger than the whole path of the log from the first run to the best — and across 37 runs the link "larger median error → more fair verdicts" gives r = +0.83. The residual on the same repeats held still: 6.4 / 6.4 / 6.8.

The error bar does not enter it at all: what is counted is the difference between price and earnings, not "can one be told from the other". A blurry run has nothing to dress itself up with. The median is used rather than the mean: for saturated traits the honest price goes past twenty points against an anti-pick ceiling of ten, and they would pull the mean entirely to themselves.

The build oracle — the breaker

Prices that are fair on average across all builds can still leave one or two combinations in the table that beat everything else. Measuring random builds cannot notice this in principle: the strongest one turns up among them once in a thousand. The oracle hunts for it on purpose.

Generations with culling

Builds play games, the worse half is culled, and its place is taken by the offspring of the survivors — the same build with one trait replaced. The offspring is legal by construction: the dropped trait is forbidden, the rest are declared mandatory, and the same generator fills up the budget — the instrument has no second set of legality rules.

Lesson
The horizon is an instrument too

With a 300-turn cap the culling was decided on games of 50, 100 and 200 turns, and sprinters survived to the final. In round 5 the leader was a build that had sold off its scientists and fighters — and a measurement at five hundred turns that same evening called "poor scientists" overpriced. It was not the instruments that disagreed, but the horizons.

Lesson
Neighbours are shuffled every generation

Strength is measured as a share, that is, always relative to whoever sits in the same game. Pin the neighbours, and the oracle starts looking not for the strongest build but for a lucky neighbourhood.

Lesson
Three builds level with each other is what balance is

The gap used to be measured against the middle of the top, and that was too strict: requiring the best not to pull away from the twentieth means requiring the choice of race to mean nothing. The real question is different: does the strongest have worthy rivals.

Lesson
A barrier that did nothing

After the culling floor the set of finalists never changes, yet the loop still went generation by generation, two tables at a time with a wait between them: in a two-hundred game run that came to about ninety generations out of a hundred. The remainder is now played as a single batch — the same games with the same neighbours, only no longer waited for two at a time.

What the oracle answers

Exactly the two conditions of the plan, and the second one with two lists, not one:

  • Is there a dominant build — the gap between the first and the third against a tolerance of one point. If the top has fewer than three builds, it counts as dominant: there is no choice there by construction.
  • Dead traits — those the search tried but which made it into no build of the top. According to the plan this means "the mechanic does not work", not "the trait is too dear".
  • Untried — those the search never put into any build of the ladder. There is nothing to say about them, and lumping them in with the dead would pass a gap in the search off as a verdict on the trait.

The oracle uses the very same measure as the price verdicts — and that is not economy but a requirement: one instrument cannot have two different measures, or the assessor and the breaker would argue not about prices but about what counts as strength.

How they close into a loop

The measurement sets prices → prices move part of the way → the oracle looks for what can be squeezed out of the new prices → its finds go into the next measurement as a planted combo → and round again. Both records live in one table of runs, differing only in kind: it is the same run of games, just with a different question. Every price change goes into the log together with what it was measured on — without the log, a month later you can neither roll back nor tell where a price came from.

A run caught by a server shutdown is closed at the next start: its thread died with the process, and left "running" it would have locked the console forever.

Key services

Game numbers
PopulationCalculator

All of MoO2's numeric rules in one place: output, growth, money, pollution, the buy-out price of construction. Formulas are changed only here.

Colonies
ColonyService

Colonist jobs, output with buildings, the build queue, selling buildings, buy-out, pollution. The queue is stored as a string in the planet itself.

Turn
TurnService + TurnContext

The galaxy is read once per turn and handed to the phases as a context. A phase that changed the state updates the context too.

Ships
ShipDesignService + ShipDesignRules

The six design slots are never empty; an update goes in as a new row and the old one is marked superseded — a built ship keeps what it was built with.

Combat
TacticalBattleService + BattleRules

A 32×20 field, ships one by one, turn order by each ship's initiative. Hits follow the original's curve; armour that is pierced knocks out a random slot.

Fleet
FleetService + FlightRules

Range from the nearest own base of support, travel time by the slowest ship. A fleet in flight is not anywhere.

Neighbours
AiEmpireService

Construction by payback, colonisation, defence by threat, buy-out for gc, espionage and subjugation by telepaths. A mechanic the AI has no path to is dead for balancing.

Balance
BalanceRunService + BalanceOracleService

The assessor and the breaker. Prices are snapshotted before the first game and sit in the run record itself: prices are edited from the neighbouring screen, and without the snapshot there is no telling what was measured.

The conventions everything rests on

  • An API refusal carries a key, not text. A single handler translates it; the substitutions are translated too. A word saved in the database in one language cannot be translated afterwards by anything.
  • The turn report is stored as keys and translated on reading. One player ends the turn, but everyone opens the report — each in their own language.
  • Text colour is a role, size is a step on the scale. Six roles with measured contrast on the main background; no ad-hoc shades anywhere in the interface.
  • Randomness is derived from the game seed everywhere — leaders, battles, research, events. Otherwise paired runs would compare not races but luck.
